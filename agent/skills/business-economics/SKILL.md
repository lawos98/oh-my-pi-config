# Business Economics

1. Define the decision, time horizon, currency, tax treatment, customer/account unit, and whether figures describe cash, recognized revenue, or profit. Use authorized supplied records; label missing inputs rather than invent them.
2. Build an input ledger: name, value or range, unit, period, source/date, and status (observed, estimated, or assumption). Keep proposed prices separate from realized prices. Do not substitute industry heuristics for evidence.
3. State formulas before calculating. Match periods and units; distinguish gross margin from contribution margin, recurring from one-time costs, and customers from subscriptions or transactions. Specify what acquisition cost and lifetime-value estimates include and the cohort/horizon they cover.
4. Reconcile totals to the source accounts: revenue less applicable costs equals the reported result; opening cash plus inflows minus outflows equals closing cash. Explain refunds, taxes, timing, noncash items, and discrepancies. Do not equate bookings, revenue, profit, and cash.
5. Compare a baseline and plausible downside/upside scenarios using explicit changed inputs. Show sensitivity to the uncertain drivers; do not present a scenario as a forecast. For runway, show the burn period and cash availability assumptions; if burn varies, calculate period-by-period rather than dividing by a misleading average.
6. Return the decision, input ledger, formulas/results, reconciliation, scenario comparison, and limits. Identify which evidence would change the recommendation. Mark unsupported outputs as unavailable, not zero.

Do not execute payments, purchases, trades, account changes, outreach, or data imports. Do not claim accounting, tax, legal, or investment approval. Escalate such decisions to the authorized responsible person; this skill provides analysis only.
